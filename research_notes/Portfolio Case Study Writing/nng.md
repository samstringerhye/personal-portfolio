# NN/g portfolio and case-study guidance

## What NN/g recommends including in a design portfolio case study

### Takeaway
For a senior visual designer, the case study should make the scope of personal contribution easy to find, explain the decisions behind the visuals, and distinguish team research and program results from work personally performed. Final screens alone cannot show that.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g's portfolio article says to decide the three takeaways a reader should retain, then make sure the portfolio delivers them. It reports a survey of 204 UX hiring professionals and says hiring managers ask for the opportunity, the value created, what was left out and why, the role, constraints, timeline, iteration, and research's influence on design. — [5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- The same article gives a seven-part case-study checklist: the problem or hypothesis; the designer's specific role and collaboration; the route to proposed solutions; how those solutions solved the problem; challenges and unpursued concepts; effect on users and business; and learning. It calls for relevant process material as well as final screens. — [5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- The article explicitly includes people who design visual or discrete interface elements in its definition of designer. It says a portfolio should showcase a range of skills when someone made varied contributions on a small number of large projects. — [5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- NN/g's homepage case study begins with design goals, then names the research and prototype-testing process, records specific design tradeoffs, and shows how a component stylesheet supported consistent implementation. The cited team used three testing rounds and sorted feedback into copy, architecture, layout, and visual style. — [Case Study: Iterative Design and Prototype Testing of the NN/g Homepage](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/case-study-iterative-design-prototyping/)
- NN/g recommends a basic, repeatable template across projects so readers can consume a portfolio coherently; it also says visual evidence and text should tell a story together. — [5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)

### Inferences
- For Samsung, the strongest three takeaways are likely: visual design built from approved wireframes, GNB hierarchy and presentation decisions, and component and template work that made those designs reusable. The case study should name research as team input and avoid saying the senior visual designer conducted it.
- A concise structure can retain NN/g's seven facts without forcing an equal section for each: a short summary and role block, then problem and constraints, selected decisions with annotated visuals, verified project impact, and learning. The visual sequence should evidence the claims: navigation imagery and type spacing with the GNB decision, page templates with the migration and hierarchy decisions, and component views with the system contribution.
- The 2021 final design handoff is an end date and delivery milestone. It can establish the project timeline and the designer's completion point, but it does not satisfy NN/g's requested effect on users or business.

### Gaps
- The reviewed NN/g guidance does not provide a special rule for a visual designer who did not own research, IA, wireframes, or launch measurement. The appropriate application is to state those boundaries plainly and credit the relevant team members or workstream.
- No verified Samsung source in this research establishes a user or business effect attributable to the senior visual design contribution. Do not fill that gap with a launch, a handoff, a component library, or a market count described as personal impact.

## How to write the page for web readers

### Takeaway
NN/g's web-writing guidance supports a fact-first, outcome-first page with informative headings, short paragraphs, captions, and a clear path to deeper evidence. It does not support promotional claims or a long chronological diary.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g reports that 79% of test participants scanned new web pages, while 16% read word by word. It recommends highlighted keywords, meaningful subheadings, bullets, one idea per paragraph, inverted-pyramid ordering, and substantially shorter text than conventional writing. — [How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)
- NN/g's succinct-writing guidance says people read more slowly on screens, skim rather than read, and benefit from short, scannable text split across topical pages. Its stated rule of thumb is to use no more than half the text used in a hard-copy publication. — [Be Succinct! (Writing for the Web)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/be-succinct-writing-for-the-web/)
- NN/g's research article reports better measured usability for concise, scannable, and objective versions of a site than for a promotional control version. It also reports that participants scanning text often read only the first sentence of each paragraph; headings, captions, topic sentences, tables of contents, bullets, and graphics helped scanning. — [Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)
- NN/g says graphical elements should complement text, and users liked summaries and inverted-pyramid presentation because it made the main point easy to find quickly. — [Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)

### Inferences
- Use direct headings that convey the decision or evidence, such as “Category imagery gave product groups a recognizable entry point,” rather than generic labels such as “The process.” If a reader scans only headings and image captions, they should still understand the role, evidence, choices, and result boundary.
- Put the verified scale or validated result in the opening only if the wording makes its level of attribution clear. A claim about phased rollout can establish project context; it cannot be worded as a user or business impact without supporting evidence.
- Captions should carry the visual claim, not merely name the screen. For example, a navigation caption should say what the image and type-spacing decision helped a shopper see or compare, while crediting the approved structure or research if applicable.

### Gaps
- The cited usability-writing research is older. Its scanning and structure guidance remains directly useful for page construction, but its percentage findings should not become generic proof that the Samsung case study itself will perform better.

## How to separate milestones, deliverables, and impact

### Takeaway
NN/g treats an outcome as a demonstrated change in user experience or business performance, measured against an appropriate baseline when possible. A completed Figma file, final handoff, design-system library, or market rollout is evidence of work completed or program scale; it is not by itself evidence that the work improved user or business outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g's case-study checklist separates “how the solution solved the problem” from “how the project affected users and the business.” Its example of the former uses follow-up usability testing; its example of the latter uses sales, app reviews, and satisfaction changes. — [5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- In NN/g's quantified case study, the team repeated the same task set after the redesign. The measured findability score moved from 4.0 to 7.4 out of 10, which NN/g reports as an 85% increase. The article then says revenue and leads rose but explicitly says the architecture's contribution could not be isolated. — [Quantifying UX Improvements: A Case Study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quantifying-case-study/)
- The quantified case study describes benchmarking as choosing metrics that represent an experience and tracking them to see how interventions affect them. — [Quantifying UX Improvements: A Case Study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quantifying-case-study/)
- NN/g's homepage exemplar frames its design goals as outcomes, not features, and uses those goals as a decision guide and a way to assess whether the work was successful. — [Case Study: Iterative Design and Prototype Testing of the NN/g Homepage](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/case-study-iterative-design-prototyping/)

### Inferences
- Treat these as different statements in the Samsung case study:
  - **Personal contribution:** turned approved wireframes into visual design for the Home, GNB, product-family, product-category, and PDP templates; shaped the GNB's visual hierarchy, image arrangement, and type spacing; created and organized reusable visual components and their files.
  - **Team or program deliverable:** a shared page-template and component system, final design handoff, and phased rollout across markets.
  - **User or business impact:** only a validated before/after user measure, analytics change, conversion or support measure, or clearly labeled stakeholder evidence. None should be claimed until a dated source establishes it.
- If no impact evidence survives, the honest closing is a result boundary: “The available project records confirm scope, delivery, and rollout; they do not provide a post-launch user or business measure that can be attributed to this work.” Then the page can close with a concrete design learning, without inventing an outcome.
- A rollout count can be phrased as scale, such as “the program's first release reached X markets,” provided the source and date are known. It should never appear under a heading that implies it measures the quality or effect of the visual designer's work.

### Gaps
- A valid Samsung impact claim requires evidence not found in these NN/g articles: pre/post usability results, post-launch analytics, a business KPI, or a client statement that specifies what changed and how it was measured. The case study should be revised if those records are found.
