# Portfolio case-study guides outside NN/g

## Key Question

Which advice from the cited non-NN/g portfolio-writing guides should shape a credible, consistent Samsung case study for a senior visual designer, and where do those guides disagree?

### Takeaway

The durable consensus is simple: make a quick reader understand the project, the designer's actual role, the decision logic, the finished work, and the verified result. Put the strongest visual evidence early, use captions and headings that carry meaning, and remove process artifacts that do not advance the story. This fits Samsung well: lead with a concise outcome or scope statement, state Sam's Senior Designer contribution precisely, show the navigation, templates, and system components near their related claims, then make the program-level rollout attribution explicit.

For this portfolio, use a **stable information architecture** across projects rather than a rigid page recipe: outcome or scope; context and constraint; role; consequential decisions; evidence of the final work; outcomes and attribution; reflection. Let each case study vary the emphasis, image sequence, and section headings according to the work. This resolves the real disagreement between template advocates (clarity and scanability) and Van Schneider/Semplice (avoid identical storytelling).

Do not import a UX process that Sam did not perform. Samsung should not claim discovery, personas, wireframes, usability testing, localization, IA, or post-launch metrics without source evidence. The strongest story is visual-system work within a global redesign: turning approved wires and inherited global rules into reusable visual components and page templates, with specific navigation decisions.

**Recommended for Samsung and the portfolio template**

- Put role, team context, timeframe, and scope near the top. State contribution with verbs that distinguish what Sam designed from work done by the wider team.
- Lead sections with informative claims, not labels such as “Process” or “Final designs.” A scanner should understand the story from headings, captions, and images alone.
- Pair each important claim with supporting evidence: GNB visuals for hierarchy, page examples for templates, and system views for components and styles. Caption what the reader should notice and why it mattered.
- Use an outcome only where it has a clear source and attribution. A phased global rollout is a program outcome; it is not evidence that Sam independently caused the rollout. A final design handoff is a project milestone, not an outcome.
- Keep copy in first person where describing Sam's work and in team language where describing shared work. Name the limits of the role without turning the case study into a list of exclusions.
- Write copy before designing the final editorial layout, then cut it until it is readable as a short visual story.

**Low-confidence or unsuitable recommendations**

- UXfolio's fixed discovery-to-validation sequence is useful only for work where the designer actually performed those steps. Its citation-free claims about what recruiters prefer, hiring outcomes, and exact word counts are marketing guidance from a portfolio-platform publisher, not independent evidence.
- “Recruiters spend less than 10 seconds” (Van Schneider) and “less than a minute” (Mayerr) are useful reminders to prioritize scanning, but neither article provides a study for those exact figures. Treat them as editorial heuristics.
- “Show an impact” is sound, but substitute a program fact, documented stakeholder response, or a candid limitation only when no user or business metric exists. Do not turn a visual difference into an unverified usability improvement.
- Advice to imitate a project's brand in every case-study page conflicts with the user's need for a coherent portfolio. Borrow the work's visual material where it explains the work; preserve the site’s editorial system.

### Cited Findings

- [Behance: “Building the Perfect Case Study,” Nick Longo, April 18, 2025](https://www.behance.net/resources/articles/building-the-perfect-case-study) proposes overview, challenge, process, solution, and reflection. Its useful contribution is the insistence that case studies explain decisions and problem solving, not just output. Use its structure as a coverage check, not an instruction to manufacture every phase.

- [Semplice: “How to write case studies for your online portfolio,” undated](https://www.semplice.com/how-to-write-case-studies-for-your-portfolio) says to draft content before layout; use short headlines, paragraphs, and captions; explain role and credits; use plain language; and give context for the approach. It also says a reader should understand the project from captions alone and recommends a roughly three-minute maximum. The guide is published by a paid portfolio product, but these specific writing principles are well supported by the page itself. Its advice to make every page unique is useful at the visual-story level, while its product promotion should carry no weight in the template decision.

- [Tobias van Schneider / DESK: “The portfolio case study is broken,” January 18, 2022](https://vanschneider.com/blog/portfolio-tips/the-old-case-study-is-not-working/) directly rejects a copied “challenge, user journey, sketches, result” formula. It recommends first distilling the story into one paragraph, centering the designer’s strengths, making visuals connect to copy, using a hook, and writing for one reader. Strong advice for the Samsung opening and imagery sequence. His exact scan-time claim is not sourced.

- [Tobias van Schneider / DESK: “Portfolios with exemplary case studies,” December 11, 2019](https://vanschneider.com/blog/portfolio-tips/portfolios-with-examplary-case-studies/) gives a practical visual rule: start by captioning the images, then let those captions shape the story. It also recommends client/team/role metadata up front and writing in a recognizably human voice. This is directly useful for auditing whether Samsung’s images earn their place.

- [TylerD&I: “An Introduction to Case Studies for Designers,” Jenny B. Kowalski, October 13, 2020](https://medium.com/tylergaid/an-introduction-to-case-studies-for-designers-80e1abc50b8f) gives a basic sequence: introduction and team, reframed problem, approach with successes and struggles, then final deliverables. It is a teaching resource aimed at students, so it overweights process for this experienced designer’s work. Keep its call for candid team credit and decision rationale; omit generic student-process artifacts.

- [Toptal: “All About Process: Dissecting Case Study Portfolios,” Adnan Puzic, last updated July 7, 2026](https://www.toptal.com/designers/ui/case-study-portfolio) defines a case study as a first-person account of the client problem, role, process, and outcome; asks for constraints, results, and learnings; warns against overlong documentation and blurred team roles; and says to use metrics only when they make sense. The article is career-platform content, but its role-clarity and truthful-metrics guidance fits Samsung especially well. Its broad formula should flex to match Sam’s visual-design scope.

- [UXfolio: “The Ultimate UX Case Study Template & Structure,” Ákos Izsák and Tibor Balázs, June 18, 2026](https://blog.uxfol.io/ux-case-study-template/) recommends hero, overview, discovery, design process, final design, impact, and learnings. Its strongest, reusable rule is to explain each named method as **why it was used, what it found, and how it changed the next decision**. The source sells a portfolio builder and repeatedly promotes its templates and editor. It is a good prompt for evidence when a method actually occurred; it is not authority to add UX research, testing, or an impact claim to Samsung.

- [Robert Mayer: “How to make a case study for your design portfolio,” August 2022](https://mayerr.com/blog/how-to-make-a-case-study-for-your-design-portfolio/) urges designers to select work that represents future ambitions, identify the reader, put story anchors first, provide concise context, identify responsibilities and collaborators, and use images to support a short story. This is a useful editorial filter for the Samsung page. The stated reader behavior is anecdotal rather than researched.

- [Format: “How To Write A Case Study For Your Design Portfolio,” Format Team, November 8, 2018](https://www.format.com/magazine/resources/design/how-to-write-design-case-study) recommends a brief client/context setup, expectations and constraints, decision rationale, challenges, and a magazine-like image-led presentation. It is a portfolio-hosting company’s content marketing, but the project-context and constraint advice remains useful. Its generic process examples should not force Samsung into a UX narrative.

- [Wix Playground: “How to write case studies for your design portfolio,” Dana Meir, July 13, 2020; updated April 16, 2024](https://www.wix.com/playground/post/how-to-write-case-studies-for-your-design-portfolio) recommends background, goal, process, results, short story structure, headings, concise copy, and captions. It explicitly asks team members to be credited. Use it for scannable text/image pairing and metadata; discount its surrounding platform promotion.

- [Wix Playground: “How to Write the Perfect Copy for Your Design Portfolio,” February 4, 2019; updated April 16, 2024](https://www.wix.com/playground/post/how-to-write-the-perfect-copy-for-your-design-portfolio) adds that a case study should sound like its author and can remain brief for non-UX work. This supports direct first-person visual-design copy rather than a formal report. It is not independent research.

- [Opendoors Careers: “How to Write a Strong Case Study for Your Portfolio in 2026,” Florian Bölter, May 2026](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/how-to-write-a-strong-case-study-for-your-portfolio-in-2026) recommends writing headings first and making each heading communicate the section’s conclusion, then curating a lighter story than the underlying project. It also recommends a reasonably consistent presentation style across the portfolio. This is especially relevant to the user’s request for a reusable template: consistent structure can coexist with different evidence and story emphasis. The newsletter sells paid reviews and mentoring, so treat advice as expert editorial opinion, not validated hiring research.

- [Opendoors Careers: “Junior Portfolio Showcase: Madhurima Chatterjee,” Florian Bölter, July 8, 2026](https://blog.opendoorscareers.com/p/junior-portfolio-showcase-madhurima-chatterjee) praises case studies structured around impact, solution, problem, and synthesis, while warning that dense diagrams and multi-screen sections can exhaust attention. It is a single reviewer’s critique of one portfolio, so use it only as a supporting example for pacing and image density.

**Overlap:** Nearly every guide asks for context, an honest role statement, a problem or goal, selected decision logic, final work, outcomes when available, short copy, and visual evidence with captions. The repeated claims are consistent enough to become template requirements.

**Real disagreement:** Behance, TylerD&I, Toptal, Wix, and UXfolio offer predictable sequences to help a reader orient. Van Schneider and Semplice argue that copying that sequence across every project creates generic work. The operational resolution is a shared **coverage model** with non-generic headings and a project-specific visual narrative. UXfolio also recommends showing discovery, flows, testing, and validation as standard chapters; that conflicts with truthful portrayal of specialist or visual-design work, including Samsung.

### Inferences

- The best title for the Samsung page will lead with a supported result or consequential scope only if it can be stated precisely. If the only verified result is program rollout, call it a team or program result in the prose; do not imply it is Sam’s individual impact.

- Samsung’s strongest repeatable story unit is: **constraint → visual decision → evidence**. Example: inherited global color/type rules and approved wires constrained the work; Sam established imagery composition and type spacing in the GNB; annotated imagery then shows the hierarchy. This lets the case study show visual judgment without borrowing unperformed UX process.

- A reusable template should reserve a final outcome block, but permit “documented program outcome and attribution” when individual business metrics are unavailable. The block should never call final handoff an outcome.

- For the final page, restrict each gallery to a question it answers. Navigation images should prove navigation hierarchy and imagery/type treatment. Page-template images should prove template reuse across home, PDP, PCP, and product-family pages. Design-system images should prove component and style construction. Remove images that only repeat the final look.

### Gaps

- The published articles do not independently verify Samsung’s rollout, market count, business effect, accessibility result, or design performance. Those claims need project records, not portfolio-writing guides.

- The guides do not settle how much content a hiring reviewer reads. Scan-time and word-count figures are largely asserted without underlying studies. The site should be reviewed on actual page readability and visual evidence after drafting.

- No source can reconstruct Sam’s exact 2020–2021 decisions. The case study should use only decisions Sam can confirm or those documented in project files, and mark team-level work accordingly.
