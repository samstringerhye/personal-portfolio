# Reference sources for case study writing

Notes and verdicts only. No article text is reproduced here.

Read status matters for how far to trust each note:
- **Full text:** read in the page's own wording (Firecrawl scrape).
- **Summary:** read as a model-written summary (WebFetch). Wording and details may be off. Check before quoting a figure or a claim.

Verdicts: useful, marginal, off-topic.

## Full text

- **Van Schneider, DESK: "How to write project case studies for your portfolio"** (useful). Draft the copy before the layout, starting with the earliest phase. Caption every image; a reader who only reads captions should still follow the project. Cover client, location, goal, your decisions, outcome, and role. Credit your role explicitly, since a bare list of names reads as suspicious. Ask "why" repeatedly and answer it. Don't pad the page with pictures and no copy.
- **Behance (Nick Longo): "Building the Perfect Case Study"** (useful). Structure: overview, challenge, process, solution, reflection. Show process artifacts such as sketches and iterations. Don't just place finals; explain why the solution works. End with what you would change. Its title is topic-based, not outcome-led.
- **TylerD&I (Jenny B Kowalski, Medium): "An Introduction to Case Studies for Designers"** (useful). Five parts: introduction, reframe the problem, explain the approach, show deliverables, conclusion. Specific claims beat vague ones ("73% preferred B" over "most preferred B"). Explain what is not on screen and why the design is the way it is. Move plain image descriptions into alt text. Edit for structure, not only proofreading, and read the piece aloud.
- **Semplice: "How to write case studies for your portfolio"** (useful). Put the type of work in the title ("ecommerce experience"). Set the goal in one sentence. Ask "why" a thousand times. Keep the whole read to about three minutes. Admit when a result wasn't a win and say what you learned. Vendor page: its own sales pitch sits beside the advice.
- **Toptal: "All About Process: Dissecting Case Study Portfolios"** (useful). Core elements: introduce the client, present the problem, recap your role and constraints, share the solution, walk the process, describe results, note learnings, conclude briefly. Keep three to five studies, readable in one to two minutes. Leave out difficult working relationships, company-specific IP, and contentious explanations of rejected ideas. Show unselected work to show range, without a fight about it. Anonymize NDA work and say what was withheld. Metrics must not mislead and must make sense to a non-expert. Testimonials and client quotes add credibility. Make the portfolio itself accessible (contrast, typography, keyboard use, WCAG 2.2). Link to live projects, and make your role clear where you didn't design everything. Its AI note: use AI for outlines, but the decisions and lessons should be in your own voice.
- **UXfolio: "The Ultimate UX Case Study Template & Structure (2026)"** (useful, with a caveat: the publisher sells a case study builder). Hero title formula: impact + scope + product. Overview covers product, role, tools, team, length, methods, and problem. Each method answers why you did it, what you found, and how it changed the next move. Final design explains why this solution and what else was considered. Impact gets its own section; without data, use before/after screens and quotes. Learnings can be takeaways or "what I'd do differently." The biggest mistake it names: defining a method instead of explaining it. Junior designers: two or three case studies. Senior: four or five. Length: about 600 words in one place, 800 to 1,500 in another. Treat length as a guideline.
- **Mayerr (Robert Mayer): "How to make a case study for your design portfolio"** (useful). Show the work you want next, and cut work that no longer matches your skills. Write for the recruiter who skims first: state the main point up front, keep it brief, and save the process detail for the interview. Context: industry, the challenge, why the project started, your role (especially if it went past scope), and who you worked with, credited by name. Visuals carry the case study; a reader may spend under a minute on it. Don't follow a standard "challenge, journey, sketch, results" formula if another shape fits the project better.
- **Format (Format Team): "How To Write A Case Study For Your Design Portfolio"** (useful). Be selective: fewer, stronger case studies. Open with client, date, and the client's expectations, including any conflicting requirements. Describe your process and why you chose it. Mention challenges, deadlines, revisions, and early drafts that show flexibility. Close with how success was measured: were the brief's requirements met, was the design used, and a client quote if you have one. Write in your usual voice, not a jargon register.
- **Wix Playground (Dana Meir): "How to write case studies for your design portfolio"** (useful). Checklist: background (client, what they do, date, location, role, team credits), goal from the brief, process and key decisions, results and learnings. Make it skimmable with headers, short paragraphs, and captions. Write it like a short story in chronological order. Its own page shows an unrendered template placeholder, so check output. Note: its chronological "story" structure is the opposite of NN/g's advice to organize by decisions.
- **NN/g (Jakob Nielsen): "Writing Style for Print vs. Web"** (useful, for copy). Print is linear and author-driven; the web is reader-driven and needs actionable content. Front-load the first words of headings and links, because readers scan. Use fragments where they carry information, and cut word count. Use specific keywords over vague labels. Users rarely read to the end of a page, so the opening has to carry the point.

## Summary only (WebFetch; verify before quoting)

- **Ellen Covey: "The Beakery"** (useful as an exemplar). Self-initiated iOS app case study. Four owner interviews, two personas, four audited adjacent services, five defined features. Two rounds of usability testing: one moderated plus seven unmoderated participants, then 14. Metadata block, a one-line goal, and jump links per design stage. Metrics are the author's own study counts. The gap to avoid: the case study states no business outcome.
- **Opendoors Careers: "How to write a strong case study for your portfolio in 2025"** (useful). Context in two or three sentences. Role, timeline, and team as a quick summary block. Put the impact in the title if you have one. Without a metric, name the effect (clearer hierarchy, less friction), or say so for student work. Organize around decisions and trade-offs, not phases. Use descriptive headings that work as a skim track. Show the work through a few key flows, not a grid of every screen, and caption every visual. Its own title is instructional, a counter-example to its advice.
- **NN/g: "Iterative Design and Prototype Testing of the Homepage"** (useful). Three outcome-focused design goals: show credibility through research, a minimal aesthetic that serves usability, and keep primary offerings reachable. Goals settled trade-offs (such as the number of colors). Three test rounds on both desktop and mobile. Feedback sorted by copy, architecture, layout, and visuals. Reported specific reversals: users preferred a list over a grid for articles. Admits a constraint: small, distributed team, low-cost tools, and a phased rollout.
- **NN/g: "Quantifying UX Improvements: A Case Study"** (useful, strongest on metrics). Client: an industrial manufacturer's website. Baseline: a tree test with 64 participants, overall score 4.0 out of 10. Redesign tested again on the same tasks: 7.4 out of 10, an 85% increase in findability. Attribution is candid: revenue and leads rose, but the architecture's share of that couldn't be isolated. Supporting evidence came from analytics. Also cites an NN/g average of 75% for UX-driven redesigns as context.
- **NN/g: "UX Design Portfolios: Top Recommendations"** (useful). Survey of 204 UX hiring professionals (cited as NN/g's finding). Steps: inventory your projects, choose three to five case studies, choose a format (web, PDF or slide deck, physical artifacts), build one template, get feedback. Seven points per case study: problem, role, solution, how it solved the problem, challenges and abandoned concepts, impact, learnings. Decide your top three takeaways before you write, then check that the portfolio delivers them. Roadblocks: under NDA, show process photos, blur sensitive details, or recreate the design generically. Short on time: pick one or two projects with broad range. Unimplemented work: show candidate solutions and the reasoning. Students: acknowledge the unrealistic parts of school projects. Its 15% sales example is hypothetical; don't use it as a benchmark.
- **NN/g: "Portfolios for UX Researchers"** (useful). Organize around what the reader needs. Three to five current projects, chosen for impact, method, and recency. Show study plans, test tasks, participant quotes, and the materials used to communicate findings. Name your role and credit collaborators. Plain language for non-specialists. One or two key findings per project. Show business impact with measurable outcomes and before/after user quotes. Acknowledge constraints: small samples, small budgets, convenience sampling. Label personal and professional work. Students and career changers can use school or volunteer projects. Under NDA, remove sensitive details, get client approval, or ask for testimonials.
- **NN/g: "Making Cutting-Edge Technology Approachable: Facial-Recognition Payment"** (useful). Opens with a real scenario and states the method plainly: five participants, two prior users, observation, before/after interviews. Organizes findings as discrete problems, each with a fix and a trade-off. Good example of a small sample reported honestly.
- **NN/g: "Writing Digital Copy for Domain Experts"** (useful, for copy). Experts scan too. Lead with facts, avoid hype, layer detail (summary first, depth below). Cite sources and link to originals. Show dates on time-sensitive content. Field terms are fine for experts; define them only for newcomers.
- **NN/g: "Iterative Design of a Survey Question"** (marginal to useful). Four revision rounds with pilot tests. Change one variable per round. Record each revision and the feedback that prompted it. Include the failed versions and what they taught. Name the participant count for each round.
- **NN/g: "Pilot Testing"** (marginal). Only transfers as one line in a case study: how the method was validated.
- **NN/g: "Tesla's Big Touchscreen"** (marginal). A critique anchored to one principle, annotated screenshots, a concrete failure scenario, and a "what works" section for balance.
- **NN/g: "Doing UX in an Agile World"** (marginal). Interviews with eight professionals. Describe deadlines and staffing that shaped the work. Report steps skipped or shortened. Cover stakeholder support or its absence.
- **NN/g: "Communicating Changes Throughout the Buyer's Journey: COVID-19"** (marginal). Open with the situation. Use numbered guidelines and annotated screenshots. Close with a reusable checklist.
- **Johnyvino.com** (marginal). Portfolio homepage, not an article. Project blurbs open with an ownership verb and the scope. The role is the first claim. Impact sits in short blurbs, not in narrative.

## Off-topic

- **NN/g: "Typography Terms: Glossary"** (off-topic for case study writing). Glossary of type terms. Presentation note only: left-aligned body text and sparing bold help scanning.
