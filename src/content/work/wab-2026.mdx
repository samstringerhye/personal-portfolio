## Three Experience Pillars

I led with three pillars that guided every design decision.

**Fun.** Confetti on completed orders. The mascot appearing in empty states. Color shifts between menu categories.

**Smart.** Surfacing orders by purchase history. Nearest-location ordering. Recent order shortcuts on the home screen.

**Intuitive.** The old ordering flow took 14 taps to get from home to checkout. The redesign cut it to 6 taps. Clear labels at every step, and progressive disclosure so customization never stacks up all at once.

</ProseGroup>

<ProseGroup>

## Research and Testing

I tested early and often. Three visual directions were pressure-tested against real wireframes to find the right balance between brand expression and daily usability.

</ProseGroup>

Direction testing identified what resonated visually: color, contrast, imagery, typography. I balanced bold, brand-forward design with readability and usability best practices. Key feature testing followed, putting landing and menu flows in front of users at critical decision moments. I carried forward the top-performing elements while refining what didn't work.

The merged direction took Vibrant Bold's iconography, typography, and color energy, then paired it with Modern's component structure and lighter palette. The result felt like Whataburger without overwhelming the daily ordering experience.

### Menu and Customization

This was the hardest flow to solve. Whataburger's customization depth (significantly more options than most QSR apps) meant the interface had to handle dense decision trees without feeling overwhelming. Branching paths (meal vs. a la carte, daypart-dependent categories, deep customization for every ingredient) meant no single pattern I found in the competitive audit covered the whole tree.

Clearer menu and customization patterns make ordering easier. Each step in the flow (browse, customize, choose sides, review) uses progressive disclosure to surface only what's relevant at that moment.

## The Design System

Before designing a single screen, I audited the existing token structure and found a system that could only describe what already existed, not what needed to be built. Primitive tokens named after the brand ("Whataburger Orange") rather than by function. No tokens for interaction states, motion, or elevation. No semantic layer. Every new screen meant inventing values from scratch.

The rebuild, codenamed Pele, delivered 258 variables across three collections. Every token reads like a path: `color/fg/primary`, `spacing/400`, `shadow/elevation/raised`. Designers get names that describe function. Engineers get names that map directly to component props without a translation layer.

</ProseGroup>

### Color States

The most critical gap in the old system was interactive states. A button had one color: no hover, no pressed, no disabled. Pele introduced 11 color states across foreground, background, and border. Every interactive element now has a complete, predictable state set.

</ProseGroup>

<ColorStates />

<ProseGroup>

### Typography

Typography follows an M3-inspired naming convention: Display, Headline, Body, Label. Display styles use Alkaline (the brand's display typeface), while all other roles use Unbounded. Each style is bound to semantic tokens for size, line-height, letter-spacing, and weight.

</ProseGroup>

<TypographyScale />

<ProseGroup>

### Spacing, Motion, and Elevation

All spatial values follow a x100 naming convention built on a 4px base. The motion system defines three duration tiers: Fast (100ms) for micro-interactions, Normal (150ms) for sheets and modals, Slow (300ms) for page transitions. Five elevation levels are composed from shadow tokens rather than arbitrary box-shadow values.

</ProseGroup>

### Components

The component library organized into universal components (ready for cross-project reuse) and screen-specific components (with a migration plan to promote them after publishing). Over 90 icons, navigation, inputs, layout, and utility components. Naming conventions mapped directly to React component props, so the dev team could pull values straight from the system.

## What I Learned

Building the system first eliminated the micro-decisions that slow every flow down. When a new screen needed a hover state or an elevation level, the answer already existed. The upfront time felt slow. The downstream speed made up for it.

Working embedded with a client UX team taught me different collaboration patterns. You earn trust through the quality of your work and the clarity of your rationale, not through org-chart authority. The closest collaboration happened during menu customization, where my visual exploration surfaced UX issues that led to meaningful flow changes. That only happens when visual design is involved early, not applied as a skin at the end.
