# Build case studies around earned evidence

The articles point to a clear direction for the Samsung case study: make the reader understand the work, Sam’s role, the visual decisions, and the evidence behind them quickly. The strongest page is not a generic UX narrative because that would misrepresent the work. It should show how a Senior Designer turned approved wireframes and Samsung’s global foundations into visual page templates, navigation treatments, and reusable components. The record supports a phased global rollout as **program context**, but it does not support a personal user or business impact claim. Final design handoff in 2021 belongs in the timeline as a delivery milestone. A consistent portfolio should reuse the same coverage model across projects while letting each project use its own headings, evidence, and visual sequence.

## NN/g’s seven questions expose the missing proof

NN/g asks a case study to answer seven questions: the problem, the designer’s role, the proposed solution, how it addressed the problem, abandoned ideas or challenges, effect on users or business, and learning. It also asks designers to show relevant process material alongside final work, because final screens alone cannot explain judgment or responsibility. This framework fits visual-design work as well as UX work; NN/g explicitly includes people who design visual or discrete interface elements in its definition of designer. ([5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/))

For Samsung, **the role and decision evidence are strong**. Sam can credibly say that he translated approved wireframes into visual designs for the homepage, GNB, PDP, PCP, and Product Family templates; shaped GNB imagery, hierarchy, and type spacing; and built reusable visual components, styles, and files within Samsung’s global system. The role block should name this work before the narrative begins. It should credit research and testing to the team, avoid claiming IA, wireframing, localization, research, or implementation, and use team language for shared work.

The missing category is **attributable impact**. NN/g separates an explanation of how a solution solved a design problem from demonstrated user or business effects. Its quantified example measures the same tasks before and after redesign, then distinguishes that usability gain from revenue and lead growth whose causes cannot be isolated. ([Quantifying UX Improvements: A Case Study](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quantifying-case-study/)) Samsung’s available materials establish delivery, rollout scale, and project scope, but not a post-launch metric tied to Sam’s work. The case study should state that boundary plainly instead of converting design handoff, a component library, or a market count into impact.

## Readers need claims before supporting detail

NN/g’s web-writing research favors concise, scannable, objective pages with meaningful subheadings, topic sentences, captions, and graphics that support the main point. One study found that 79% of participants scanned new pages, and its recommendations include front-loading conclusions and splitting content into short units. ([How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)) Its later writing guidance similarly found better measured usability for concise, scannable, and objective versions of a site than for a promotional version. ([Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/))

This means the Samsung headings should communicate decisions, not label phases. “Imagery and spacing clarified the GNB hierarchy” tells a reader what the work did; “Navigation” does not. “Shared components carried the visual rules across page templates” is stronger than “Design system.” A caption should tell the reader what to inspect and why it mattered: the image treatment and type spacing in the GNB, the progression from an approved structure to a visual template, or the reusable styles that kept page design consistent. The imagery should sit beside the claim it proves, not collect at the end as a gallery of finished screens.

The current materials support three visual chapters. The GNB images should show hierarchy, imagery composition, and type spacing. The page-template images should show how the visual system carried across the homepage, PDP, PCP, and Product Family pages. The system views should show the type, color, and reusable atomic components Sam built and organized. Each image earns its place when its caption makes one of those claims visible.

## Use a coverage model, not a fixed recipe

The non-NN/g guides agree on the fundamentals: establish context, identify the actual role and collaborators, explain a selected set of decisions, show final work, and report verified results. Semplice advises drafting content before layout and writing captions that can carry the story; Van Schneider similarly argues for starting with the story and allowing visuals and copy to reinforce one another. ([Semplice: How to write case studies for your online portfolio](https://www.semplice.com/how-to-write-case-studies-for-your-portfolio)) ([The portfolio case study is broken](https://vanschneider.com/blog/portfolio-tips/the-old-case-study-is-not-working/))

They disagree about uniformity. Template guides favor a recurring sequence because it makes pages easy to scan. Van Schneider warns that a copied discovery-to-solution formula produces generic portfolios. The useful resolution is a **shared coverage model**: opening claim and scope, context and constraint, role, decisive work, evidence, documented outcomes with attribution, and reflection. Those subjects should recur across the portfolio; the section names, number of sections, and image rhythm should change according to the project.

That model keeps the Samsung story honest. Its primary narrative unit is **constraint, visual decision, evidence**: inherited global type and color rules plus approved wireframes set the constraint; Sam made the GNB imagery, hierarchy, and spacing decisions; annotated navigation visuals show the result. A second unit can show the page-template work, and a third can show the reusable system work. This demonstrates senior visual-design judgment without adding discovery, prototype testing, or validation chapters that did not occur in Sam’s role.

## Delivery and scale need their own labels

The articles consistently treat an outcome as an observed change, not the existence of a deliverable. NN/g’s homepage case study sets outcome-oriented goals and assesses them through research and prototype testing; its portfolio guidance asks separately for effects on users and the business. ([Case Study: Iterative Design and Prototype Testing of the NN/g Homepage](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/case-study-iterative-design-prototyping/)) This gives the Samsung page a useful editorial distinction: contribution, deliverable, program scale, and impact are four different facts.

Sam’s contribution is the visual design, navigation presentation, templates, and reusable component work. The team deliverables are the shared design system, page templates, and final design handoff in 2021. The phased rollout across markets is program scale and should be written in team language, with a source and date wherever it appears. **None of those proves an attributable user or business result.** A final outcome block should therefore say that the available project record confirms scope, delivery, and rollout, while it does not provide a post-launch measure of user or business effect attributable to this contribution. If a dated analytics report, comparable usability study, or client statement emerges later, that block can be replaced with a measured result and its attribution.

## Conclusion

The research supports a tighter Samsung case study, not a larger one. Its authority comes from showing a specific visual-design role and the decisions that only someone in that role could explain. The strongest opening will frame the redesign’s global scale as project context, then immediately define Sam’s contribution and use annotated evidence to make the work legible.

The reusable template should preserve a place for outcomes while refusing to manufacture them. That protects the credibility of Samsung now and makes future case studies stronger when they do have validated product, user, or business evidence.
